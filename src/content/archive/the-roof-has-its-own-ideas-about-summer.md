  ### The Predicament

  The building sector across the Po Valley in Northern Italy faces major challenges with summer overheating and high cooling demands. These problems are driven by intense urban heat and an aging housing stock. In the municipality of Reggio Emilia, two multi-story social housing complexes were selected as pilot sites for an empirical study managed by municipal agencies and technical research partners. The sites were identified as **Site A on Via Guicciardini** and **Site B on Via Tiziano**.

  Before any renovation work began, building audits were carried out using continuous multi-sensor monitoring. The results revealed poor thermal performance in the building envelopes at both sites. Baseline thermal transmittance values were **1.45 W/m²K at Site A** and **1.48 W/m²K at Site B**. These high values reflected the lack of effective roof insulation beneath the existing non-ventilated clay tile coverings.

  Monitoring data also showed that indoor temperatures in top-floor apartments frequently exceeded acceptable adaptive comfort limits during the peak summer months of July and August. Temperatures rose by up to **4.5°C above these limits at Site A** and **4.2°C at Site B**. At the same time, peak temperatures on the southern roof tiles reached **68.5°C at Site A** and **69.1°C at Site B**. This intense heat was transferred directly into the buildings through the structural ceiling slabs.

  Before the retrofit, annual cooling energy demand was **58.4 kWh/m² at Site A** and **61.2 kWh/m² at Site B**. As a result, residents relied heavily on energy-intensive portable and localized air-conditioning units to stay cool. This increased electricity demand and contributed to frequent grid overloads. It also worsened summer energy poverty, particularly among vulnerable residents living on the top floors of both buildings.

  ---

  ### The Proposed Solution

  To reduce overall cooling-energy demand and limit severe summer overheating, the research consortium developed and implemented an innovative passive roof retrofit strategy at both sites. The approach was designed to improve thermal performance without modifying the existing load-bearing timber structures. The intervention involved removing the old roofing materials and installing a new, highly ventilated roof system with air-permeable tiles.

  A secondary ventilation cavity beneath the tiles was incorporated into the roof assembly. This cavity was designed to promote continuous airflow through the stack effect, allowing accumulated solar heat to escape before it could penetrate the building structure. Both Site A and Site B received a continuous **120-millimeter layer of high-density wood-fiber insulation**, with a thermal conductivity of **0.038 W/mK**. The insulation was installed directly above the timber roof deck to reduce heat transfer through the roof.

  The outer layer consisted of micro-perforated interlocking clay tiles. Their controlled air permeability improved convective heat dissipation at the roof's external surface, helping to limit heat accumulation during periods of intense solar radiation. Dynamic energy simulations projected a reduction in the roof's steady-state thermal transmittance (U-value) to **0.22 W/m²K at Site A** and **0.21 W/m²K at Site B**.

  The installation took place during the spring and involved external scaffolding and dry-fixing mounting rails. This mounting system was selected to minimize thermal bridging, which can occur when metal fasteners penetrate the insulation layer and create pathways for heat transfer. By maintaining insulation continuity while integrating the ventilated roofing system, the retrofit aimed to improve thermal resistance and reduce summer cooling demand without requiring structural alterations to either building.

  ---

  ### Measured Data & Empirical Validation

  Post-occupancy evaluation data gathered over a subsequent two-year monitoring campaign validated substantial performance changes across both Reggio Emilia pilot blocks:

  - **Ceiling Surface Temperatures:** Peak interior ceiling plaster temperature dropped by an average of **6.2°C at Site A** and **5.9°C at Site B** during peak summer heatwaves.
  - **Cooling Energy Demand:** Annual cooling demand across monitored top-floor apartments registered a **net reduction of 48.5%** down to **30.1 kWh/m² at Site A**, while Site B saw a **reduction of 46.2%** down to **32.9 kWh/m²**.
  - **Solar Heat Discharge:** The sub-tile ventilated cavity successfully discharged approximately **35% of total incident solar radiation** via continuous kinetic buoyancy flow before thermal conduction could reach interior spaces.
  - **Thermal Flux:** Daily heat gain analyses demonstrated a **17.2% decrease in thermal flux** for Site A configurations compared against standard unventilated reference roofs.

  #### Operational Challenges & Hygroscopic Monitoring
  High-resolution hygroscopic monitoring indicated that during transitional autumn months at Site A, the increased air permeability of the sub-tile layer induced localized condensation risks near the eaves due to unmanaged vapor diffusion paths. This required retrofitting a high-performance breathable vapor-control membrane directly beneath the counter-battens of Site A.

  #### Economic Payback
  Capital cost recovery calculations derived from the empirical dataset established a simple payback period of **8.4 years for Site A** and **8.9 years for Site B**, confirming the overall technical viability and financial scalability of the thermo-roof intervention for wider municipal deployment across Mediterranean residential stocks.

By the third week of July, I had begun to take my ceiling personally.

It was not a ceiling one could resent quietly. It announced itself at eight in the evening, exactly when the street below had finally exhaled and the church bell at San Pietro had finished counting out the hour. I would lie on top of the sheets, watching the plaster darken the way plaster does when it is holding something in, and I would think: you have been doing this since five o'clock this morning, and you are still not finished.

My grandmother, who raised five children in this apartment on Via Guicciardini, used to say that a house learns its habits the way a person does, slowly and without consulting anyone. She was right. Our building had learned to hoard the afternoon the way a miser hoards coins. Its clay tiles had spent the day drinking sunlight, and its timber bones had soaked up what the tiles passed down, and by the time the good citizens of Reggio Emilia were sitting down to tortelloni the whole structure was returning its investment to whoever happened to be underneath. There is a word architects use for this: thermal mass. I know this now because a young engineer with kind eyes and a clipboard came to my door in June and explained that my ceiling had, thermally speaking, the transmittance of a piece of toast. One point four five watts per square metre per kelvin, he said, writing it down for me, as though the number itself might cool the room.

I asked him if that was bad. He looked at the ceiling and then at me, and in the silence I understood that Reggio Emilia's engineers are raised to be polite.

Upstairs, the roof was enjoying itself. The sensors the engineers had persuaded us to host recorded the southern tiles at 68.5 degrees on the afternoon that would live in my memory. Sixty-eight and a half. I asked the engineer what that was in real terms and he considered the matter and said that a properly motivated frying pan operates at a lower temperature. Down in my bedroom the air sat four and a half degrees above what any humane standard of comfort would allow, and it sat there, one should add, with the settled patience of a cat that has chosen your chair.

My neighbour on Via Tiziano, Giacomo, whose building had been built by the same hands in the same decade and had grown up with the same bad habits, was enduring a close variation of my own education. His tiles topped mine by six tenths of a degree, which he mentioned whenever the subject arose, in the tone of a man who has won something. His ceiling overshot comfort by 4.2 degrees, and we agreed, across the courtyard wall, that we were living inside a memory of the afternoon while the evening stood politely outside, unable to get in.

The portable air-conditioner was my summer companion. It arrived one June in a box large enough to house a child and settled into the corner of the room with the air of a machine that intends to be useful and expensive. It hummed. It dripped onto the balcony in a way the neighbours found expressive. And it ate, the way these machines do, consuming fifty-eight point four kilowatt-hours per square metre of my apartment across the year, which is a figure I would not know except that the clipboard kindly translated it into euros at the end of August, at which point I requested a glass of water and a moment alone.

Giacomo was worse off. Sixty-one point two. He kept his bills in a folder marked with a drawing of a flame.

There is something absurd, and I say this with love, in paying money every summer to persuade one's own ceiling to stop impersonating a radiator. The money went out. The heat came in. The grid of our town strained like a mule, and somewhere far away a power station coughed into the haze on my behalf.

I will not pretend I greeted the engineers' return with open arms. Men with clipboards have been the ruin of many a quiet afternoon, and when they reappeared in October with drawings of my roof I assumed the worst. But what they proposed turned out to have the charm of a very old idea wearing new clothes.

Above the timber deck, which had carried that roof for longer than anyone living could remember, they laid one hundred and twenty millimetres of wood-fibre insulation pressed as dense as good bread, a material that conducts heat about as willingly as I conduct exercise. Over this went the clever part. They raised the tiles on rails, dry-fixed so that no metal bolt would stab through the insulation and carry heat the way a wire carries news, and beneath those tiles they left a gap. An open corridor. A lane for air.

Now, a chimney has understood the relevant principle since before anyone thought to write it down, which is that warm air rises and will gladly take a road if one is offered. Our new roof offered one. The sun still struck the tiles. The tiles still grew hot, and the hot air trapped beneath them went climbing up the channel instead of leaning down into my bedroom. A roof in Reggio Emilia has always shed water. Ours now, at last, shed summer too.

The drawings promised new U-values of 0.22, and Giacomo's building 0.21, numbers that meant nothing to me except in the way a promise means something. Roofs, like men, should be judged after some seasons have tested them.

So we waited, and the engineers returned with their sensors, and this is what two summers established. The peak temperature of the interior plaster at my flat fell by six point two degrees. Giacomo's fell by five point nine, which he accepted with grace, or at least with the grumbling by which our street communicates affection. My cooling demand dropped by nearly half, to thirty point one kilowatt-hours per square metre, and his to thirty-two point nine. The folder with the flame drawing was retired to a drawer.

I confess I still did not entirely understand until one evening in late July when I realised I was cold. Cold, in my own bedroom, in July, and reaching for a shirt. The roof was discharging a third of the sun's assault before the heat could press any further inward, and the daily gain through the whole assembly had fallen by more than seventeen per cent against the old arrangement. My ceiling had become, after all these years, a ceiling again. Something that sits above a room and minds its own business.

Not everything about a building yields to a good intention. In the damp weeks of autumn the sensors whispered of condensation gathering near the eaves, where the newly generous passage of air had revealed that moisture had its own routes and its own timetable, and that nobody had consulted us about either. I learned from this that a roof is two problems stitched together, and the second one is the one you forgot. A breathable membrane beneath the counter-battens now stands guard where the vapour would creep in, and the matter was settled quietly, the way sensible matters in houses ought to be.

The money, when the clipboard finally closed its cover, told a decent story. Eight and a half years for my building to earn back what was spent, eight point nine for Giacomo's, against a lifetime of timber above and people below that runs far longer. A decade is a long time to a tenant and a short time to a roof, and every roof in the world knows which of those it intends to outlast.

What pleases me, and here I will allow myself the pleasure, is how little we disturbed. The clay tiles of Reggio Emilia still lie where clay tiles have lain for centuries, doing the first of their two old jobs. Beneath them rests the wood fibre, patient as the timber it guards. Between the two, a gap of measured air does a small share of the work, unpaid and unnoticed, like everyone who actually keeps this town running. No one has hung a plaque. No committee came. Architecture has grander subjects and handsomer photographs, and the people who sleep under roofs are rarely asked to speak at the ceremonies.

But I will say this, since the plaque is never coming. At Via Guicciardini, and at Via Tiziano where Giacomo keeps score, July now enters the apartments the way a guest should, politely, and leaves when asked. The ceiling over my bed minds its manners. The machine in the corner, I have noticed, switches itself off some evenings and simply rests, which is more than I could manage myself at its age.

The roof has always had the final word in summer. In Reggio Emilia it simply says something kinder now, and says it more quietly, and lets a man sleep.
